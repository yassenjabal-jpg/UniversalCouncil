# Studio Pilot 001 — W2 S0 Story Question

Status: ACTIVE  
Entry gate: **OWNER_TITLE_LOCK**  
Selected story: **P2 — North Korea's remote-worker laptop farms**  
Locked title: **لماذا احتاجت كوريا الشمالية 187 لابتوب داخل بيوت أمريكية؟**

## Core story question

**كيف يمكن لشركة أن تظن أن موظفها يعمل من داخل الولايات المتحدة، بينما الموجود فعليًا داخل أمريكا هو اللابتوب فقط؟**

## Story promise

The episode will reconstruct the mechanism behind alleged DPRK remote-IT-worker laptop-farm schemes: why physical U.S.-based laptops mattered, how identity and location signals could be made to look legitimate to employers, how the arrangement reached real companies, and why authorities treated it as more than ordinary remote-work fraud.

## Answer hypothesis to test in research

The scheme works because remote hiring systems often trust a bundle of signals that can be separated from the actual human being:

1. an identity presented to the employer;
2. a company-issued laptop physically located in the expected country;
3. network/location signals generated from that laptop;
4. remote-control infrastructure that lets another person operate it from elsewhere;
5. local intermediaries or facilitators who receive, host or manage devices.

The research phase must test each link independently. The final script must not imply that every case used the exact same mechanics unless primary evidence supports that claim.

## Narrative engine

**Normal remote job → one strange laptop → hidden domestic laptop farm → remote operator elsewhere → money and access flowing through the arrangement → law-enforcement discovery → what this exposes about remote hiring.**

The story should progress as an investigation, not as a list of cyber-security facts.

## First-30-second viewer question

A company ships a brand-new work laptop to an address in America.

The laptop turns on in America.
Its internet traffic appears to come from America.
The employee logs in every day.

**So how can the employee be thousands of kilometres away?**

The answer begins with the fact that, in this scheme, the laptop and the worker did not need to be in the same country.

## Key reveal ladder

R1 — The “employee location” signal can come from the device, not necessarily the person.

R2 — Employer laptops can be physically hosted in ordinary U.S. residences.

R3 — Remote-access tooling can allow the real operator to use those machines from elsewhere.

R4 — Identity, payroll and hiring workflows can become separate attack surfaces.

R5 — The alleged objective is not merely salary fraud; authorities also frame these schemes as sanctions evasion, revenue generation and potential access into corporate systems.

R6 — The larger story is about a weakness created when companies treat digital presence, physical device location and human identity as if they were the same thing.

## Human stakes

The episode should make the risk understandable without turning it into abstract “cyber war”:
- a legitimate company thinks it hired a normal remote employee;
- another person's identity may be used;
- a homeowner or facilitator may physically host the machines;
- corporate systems and data may become reachable;
- money can leave ordinary payroll systems and enter a sanctioned network.

## Visual anchor

The recurring visual object is the **company laptop**.

It begins as a normal work-from-home device, then becomes the physical disguise that lets the audience understand the entire mechanism.

## What the episode is NOT

- not “North Koreans are hackers”;
- not a generic history of North Korea;
- not a broad cyber-security explainer;
- not a tutorial for evading employer controls;
- not a claim that every remote worker from a particular country is suspicious;
- not a dramatized accusation beyond what primary evidence supports.

## Research questions for S1

1. What exactly did the 2026 DOJ actions allege and what was already proven in earlier cases?
2. What does “laptop farm” mean operationally in the cited cases?
3. Why was physical device presence in the United States useful?
4. Which identity, hiring, payroll and remote-access techniques are documented in primary sources?
5. What role did U.S.-based facilitators allegedly play?
6. How many companies/devices are tied specifically to the October 2026 action, and which numbers belong to broader multi-year enforcement?
7. What harms are documented: wages, sanctions evasion, data access, malware, extortion, or other activity?
8. Which claims come from indictments/complaints versus convictions or admitted facts?
9. What defensive lesson can employers learn without the episode turning into procedural evasion guidance?
10. Which details are visually provable with court exhibits, DOJ imagery, device photos, diagrams or reporting footage?

## S0 exit test

PASS only if S1 can support:
- a clean mechanism;
- a documented sequence of events;
- a legally careful distinction between allegations and proven facts;
- enough primary evidence for an 8+ minute documentary;
- a visual story centered on the laptop rather than generic hacker imagery.

If any of those fail, return to W1 through a formal title/story revision request. The locked title cannot change automatically.
